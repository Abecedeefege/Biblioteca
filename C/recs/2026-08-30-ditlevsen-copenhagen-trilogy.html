For you, C · New · Your second Ditlevsen

The Copenhagen Trilogy

Tove Ditlevsen · 1967–1971 · “Childhood · Youth · Dependency”

Three very short books about one working-class girl from Vesterbro who decided, around the age of ten, that she was going to be a poet — and about everything that decision cost. Written in a flat, unhurried present tense that never once asks you to feel sorry for her. You already own her short stories. This is the thing they were orbiting.

Why it came to you

You already bought the wrong Ditlevsen first

Not wrong exactly — but The Trouble with Happiness on your A2 shelf is the late short stories, the offcuts. The Copenhagen Trilogy is the book everyone actually means when they say her name, and it lands in the middle of a triangle you have already built without noticing: Ernaux, who turned her own life into documentary evidence; Cusk, whose Outline works by subtraction; Plath, whom you own twice over and once in biography. Ditlevsen's German publisher calls her the trailblazer for exactly those first two, and they are not wrong. She got there in 1967, in a language almost nobody outside Denmark was reading.

The keys

One line from the book

The opening — and why it tells you everything

“In the morning there was hope. It sat like a fleeting gleam of light in my mother's smooth black hair that I never dared touch; it lay on my tongue with the sugar and the lukewarm oatmeal I was slowly eating while I looked at my mother's slender, folded hands that lay motionless on the newspaper, on top of the reports of Spanish flu and the Treaty of Versailles.”

Tove Ditlevsen, opening lines of Childhood (1967), translated by Tiina Nunnally

Spanish flu and Versailles are on the table because the mother is reading them, not because the memoirist is establishing period. That is the whole method in one sentence: history stays where a child would have found it — underneath a pair of hands, at breakfast.

The record card

Facts before you decide

Originally
Three separate Danish books: Barndom and Ungdom (both 1967), Gift (1971)
In English
Penguin Classics collected all three as The Copenhagen Trilogy in 2019; Farrar, Straus and Giroux published the US edition on 26 January 2021
Translators
Childhood and Youth by Tiina Nunnally; Dependency by Michael Favala Goldman
Earlier English
Nunnally's first two volumes came out in 1985 from Seal Press as Early Spring — same text, older packaging, don't buy it twice
In French
Globe, Paris, in a new translation by Christine Berlioz and Laila Flink Thullesen: Enfance (2023), then Jeunesse and Dépendance — sold as separate slim volumes, easy to find where you are
In German
Aufbau, translated by Ursel Allenstein: Kindheit, Jugend, Abhängigkeit, also available as a boxed trilogy
Recognition
One of The New York Times' 10 Best Books of 2021, and No. 71 on the same paper's 100 Best Books of the 21st Century (2024)
The author
Tove Ditlevsen, 1917–1976, born and died in Copenhagen; began writing poems at ten, published 29 books, and was one of Denmark's best-known writers by the end of her life
Length
Short. The French Enfance runs about 160 pages; the three together are a long weekend, not a project

The honest fine print

Three warnings, and one of them is about me

Honesty guard

The third volume is genuinely hard. Dependency is where the marriages fail and the addiction takes over: a doctor husband who keeps her supplied with pethidine, abortions, years lost to the drug. She died by suicide in 1976, an overdose of sleeping pills, and the book is read in that light whether or not you want it to be. If you are not in the mood for that, read Childhood alone — it works completely on its own and is under two hundred pages.

It is a memoir, with a memoirist's licence. Dialogue from 1925 is reconstructed, not transcribed, and Ditlevsen shapes her own life the way a novelist would. Your historian's shelf will want to know which parts are evidence. The honest answer is: none of it, in the archival sense — and she never claims otherwise. That is a different contract than the one Hartman offered you last week, and worth noticing side by side.

My comparisons are secondhand on one point. The Ernaux and Cusk lineage is how her German publisher and much of the press frame her; it is a critical claim, not a documented influence, and Ditlevsen was dead before either of them published the books you own. Read it as a family resemblance, not a debt.
