For you, C · New · International Booker 2024

Kairos

Jenny Erpenbeck · 2021 (English translation by Michael Hofmann, 2023)

East Berlin, 1986. Katharina is nineteen; Hans is a married writer in his fifties. Their affair burns through the last years of a country that is also about to end — and the novel refuses to let you untangle the two collapses. In 2024 it became the first German novel ever to win the International Booker Prize.

Why it came to you

Your shelves have been circling this book for years

Your library keeps three threads that almost never meet in one novel: German history read seriously (Assmann on the long shadow of the past, Bessel's Germany 1945, Judt & Snyder in German), autofiction that turns a life into an era (Ernaux's The Years, Ali Smith's seasons), and literary fiction with real formal nerve (Batuman, Rooney, Stoner). Kairos is where those three threads tie: a love story told with a historian's sense of what an ending is, by a writer who grew up in the GDR and watched her country vanish at 22.

Your Assmann: Der lange Schatten der Vergangenheit (A3) Your Ernaux: The Years, La place (B4 · A2) Your Bessel: Germany 1945 (B5) Reads beautifully in the German original, too

Checked against your full catalog: no Erpenbeck on your shelves yet — this would be the first.

The image the whole novel hangs on

Jenny Erpenbeck, Kairos (tr. Michael Hofmann)

The honest fine print

This is not a comfort read

Honesty guard

Two things, upfront. First: the relationship at the centre is deeply unequal — an older man with power and a teenager — and the book stares at the control and cruelty in it without flinching; if you want warmth, keep this for another season. Second: it is interior and slow-burning, closer to your Stoner and The Years than to a plot-driven novel. That is exactly its power — but only when you're in the mood for it.
