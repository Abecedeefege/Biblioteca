For you, C · New · NBCC Award for Criticism

Wayward Lives, Beautiful Experiments

Saidiya Hartman · 2019 · “Intimate Histories of Riotous Black Girls, Troublesome Women, and Queer Radicals”

Philadelphia and New York, roughly 1890 to 1935. The only surviving records of these young Black women are the ones written by the people who policed them: arrest sheets, reformatory case files, rent collectors' notes, sociological surveys. Hartman reads that archive against itself and finds, underneath the charge sheets, a social revolution nobody bothered to write down.

Why it came to you

The book where your two best walls argue with each other

Your feminism wall already knows what happened to these women: Stella Dadzie on enslaved women's refusals, Dorothy Roberts on the policing of Black women's bodies, Angela Davis, Audre Lorde, Mikki Kendall. And your historian's shelf already knows the harder question — E. H. Carr on what a fact even is, Koselleck on layered time, Said on who gets to narrate, and, three shelves over, the Princeton Guide to Historical Research telling you to stay inside your sources. Hartman puts those two shelves in the same room and refuses to let either one win easily: what do you do when the only archive of a life is the file kept by the people who arrested her?

One line from the book

Her definition of the word in the title

“Waywardness is a practice of possibility at a time when all roads, except the ones created by smashing out, are foreclosed. It obeys no rules and abides no authorities.”

Saidiya Hartman, from the chapter “Wayward: A Short Entry on the Possible”

“Wayward minor” was a real legal category — it is how the state described a girl who might commit a crime later. Hartman takes the word away from the courts and gives it back to the women.

The honest fine print

Three warnings, and one of them is the point

Honesty guard

It breaks the rule your Princeton Guide teaches. Where the file goes silent, Hartman writes in the subjunctive — what might have been said, what the room probably smelled like, what the woman may have wanted. She says so openly and calls the method critical fabulation. If you want history that never steps past what a document can prove, this book will irritate you for four hundred pages. I'd argue that irritation is the most useful thing on this list: it is the same fight your Carr picked, taken one step further than he took it.

The material is heavy. Arrests, reformatories, sexual violence, tenements, the long reach of the vagrancy and wayward-minor laws. It is not grim for its own sake — the argument is about joy and refusal — but it does not spare you.

It is not linear. Chapters run from a single page to forty, photographs and case files sit next to lyric passages, and the same women return under different lights. Read it in sittings, not in one gulp.
